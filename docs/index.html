Method & sources

Inputs, assumptions and validation

Separate the supplied information, the completion policy and the numerical evidence supporting the result.

Specified information

The inputs are ordinary Pearson correlations. Observed entries, including specified zeros, are fixed constraints. Missing entries are completion variables. The default rounded GIRO example has five variables, six supplied pairs and four missing pairs.

The full-precision notebook preset is separate from the rounded slide preset. Its completed 1–3 covariance is −16.7780, corresponding to an ordinary correlation of −0.4601.

Completion policies

Explicit vine: choose the vine structure and free partial correlations, then inspect the reverse-partial reconstruction. The default GIRO walkthrough uses explicit zero-partial assumptions. Arbitrary zero assignments need not be valid or optimal.

Maximum determinant: maximise the determinant subject to the observed-entry constraints. The solver supports chordal observation graphs. With Gaussianity and fixed variances, this is also maximum differential entropy. Changing the vine representation leaves the optimum unchanged.

Validity and optimality

Inspect the minimum eigenvalue, observed-entry error and log determinant. For maximum determinant under the original observed-entry constraints, the inverse correlation matrix should be zero at the free positions.

A positive definite completion supports the stated numerical assumptions. It does not establish statistical recovery, an insurance calibration or tail dependence. General nonchordal maximum-determinant optimisation and singular positive semidefinite completion are outside the solver’s scope.

Where this demonstration stops +

The explorer accepts 3–7 variable correlation matrices with arbitrary missing pairs. The displayed process follows pages 3–7 of the GIRO presentation. The slide preset uses the rounded numbers displayed on page 3, so its diagnostics differ slightly from the full-precision notebook preset. General nonchordal completion and singular positive semidefinite solutions are outside scope; a rejection here does not prove that every possible completion is impossible.

A correlation matrix does not establish a true dependence structure, marginal distributions or non-Gaussian tail dependence. Partial correlation is not generally conditional correlation. Only with an additional jointly Gaussian assumption can the three-variable 0.4 partial correlation parameterise the Gaussian conditional copula; the conditional copula and partial copula coincide in that specific model.

Validation is numerical, at stated tolerances, and needs care near singularity. Display values are rounded; checks use full precision. The example has no out-of-sample test or empirical insurance calibration.

Technical summary

Correlation-matrix completion

The demonstration separates observed correlations from added completion assumptions. A vine provides a sequence of partial-correlation assignments and numerical reconstruction steps. The completed matrix is checked for positive definiteness and preservation of the supplied entries.

In the three-variable example, ρ12 = ρ23 = 0.5 and the chosen partial correlation ρ13·2 = 0.4 imply ρ13 = 0.55. Maximum determinant under only the two observed correlations instead gives ρ13 = 0.25. The distinction is the additional assumption.

A completed correlation matrix does not identify margins or a copula family. Business implications require the wider dependence model.

Notes and references +

  1. Information and feasibility

    There are n(n−1)/2 distinct off-diagonal correlations. Supplied pairs are fixed constraints; missing pairs remain to be selected by the completion rule and any modelling judgements. The counts describe available information, not estimation accuracy.

    Apply a valid input to see its feasibility checks.

    For a chordal observed graph, positive definite fully specified maximal blocks (cliques) ensure that a positive definite completion exists. For a nonchordal graph, these block checks alone do not establish feasibility. This explorer’s maximum-determinant solver supports chordal graphs. An unsupported calculation is reported explicitly.

    References: Grone et al. (1984); Kurowicka & Cooke (2006), §4.2.

  2. Regular vine construction, labels and partial correlations

    First tree. T1 has the original n variables as nodes. Connect them with n−1 edges to make one connected tree without cycles.

    Higher trees. The edges of T(k−1) become the nodes of Tk. Each Tk must again be a connected tree without cycles: it has n−k+1 nodes and n−k edges. Continue to T(n−1), which has one edge.

    Proximity rule. From T2 onwards, two nodes may connect only if the edges they represent share a node in the preceding tree. For example, the T1 edges 12 and 24 may connect in T2, giving the label 14;2.

    Step 2 constructs Greedy, C-vine or D-vine trees automatically. Manual connections lets you edit the edges, with the same rules checked before continuing. The expansion strategy selects connections; Step 3 assigns the free partial-correlation values.

    The conditioned pair comes before the semicolon and the conditioning set after it. The separator is omitted for an empty conditioning set. Higher-tree nodes represent preceding-tree edges; connected edges must share a preceding-tree node (proximity). Graph coefficient subscripts follow this paper notation. The accompanying equations use the project’s dot notation for partial correlations.

    A partial correlation concerns linear regression residuals. It is not generally a correlation conditional on a fixed value. Free partial values are modelling judgements in explicit-vine mode and are derived from the selected optimum in maximum-determinant mode. A chosen set can conflict with the supplied correlations.

    Reference: Kurowicka & Cooke (2006), Definitions 3.1–3.2 and §2.1.

  3. Validity, objective and practical scope

    The checks establish numerical positive definiteness and preservation of supplied entries. A maximum-determinant certificate concerns that objective under the supplied-entry constraints; it does not show closeness to an unknown true matrix. The Gaussian entropy interpretation additionally needs a Gaussian model and fixed variances.

    Inputs here are illustrative. The demo has no empirical insurance calibration or out-of-sample validation. A correlation matrix alone does not identify marginal distributions, a copula family or tail dependence. The app reports unsupported choices without changing the requested method.

    See Method & sources and Grone et al. (1984) for the determinant criterion.

    Back to the linked text ↑

  4. Generating-reference comparison

    Generated examples keep a full reference matrix separate from solver inputs. The comparison measures agreement with that synthetic reference; the selected objective need not recover it. Editing supplied values may make the original reference incompatible with the current constraints. Hidden reference values are excluded from solver requests.

    Back to the linked text ↑

  5. Maximum determinant: meaning and use

    Among positive definite correlation matrices matching the supplied entries, maximise det(R), equivalently log det(R). This selects a unique completion when the feasible set is nonempty. It provides a defined, reproducible criterion when further free partial values are not imposed; choosing that criterion is itself a modelling judgement.

    For a Gaussian vector with fixed variances, differential entropy differs from ½ log det(R) by a constant. Maximising determinant therefore maximises that Gaussian entropy. This interpretation requires the Gaussian model. It does not establish closeness to an unknown true matrix or imply all missing ordinary correlations are zero.

    At the optimum, the inverse correlation matrix is zero at unspecified-entry positions. The displayed certificate checks this condition. This implementation computes the chordal solution; changing only the displayed vine representation leaves the optimum unchanged.

    References: Grone et al. (1984); Kurowicka & Cooke (2006), §4.2; Vandenberghe, Boyd & Wu (1998), §§2.2 and 2.6.
